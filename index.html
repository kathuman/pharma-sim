How to read it: what the results mean

Fill rate is the share of market demand that reached patients. In pharma the target is usually 98–99.5%, so a fraction of a point is a real shortage. Service slips before the stock runs out, because stock is spread over many presentations and markets.

Bullwhip is how much more production varies than demand (standard deviation of production ÷ standard deviation of demand). Above 1, the plant amplifies what the market does: it is chasing its own stock gap, not just demand. With a distributor, the same ratio for its orders shows where the amplification starts.

Waste is batches rejected in QA plus stock written off because too little shelf life remained for customers to accept it. With a long shelf life it is almost all rejections; with a short one, an overshoot after a surge becomes expired stock.

Profit is the plant's view: revenue minus materials, the workforce (paid whether busy or not), hiring, overtime, holding, disposal, expediting and a penalty for each pack not supplied. The figures are illustrative, chosen for a mid-priced branded medicine.

The baseline is a saved run for comparison. It uses the same random demand as the current run, so every difference is due to what you changed, not to luck. New demand future draws another random future for both.

What the model leaves out
  • One product family, one plant and at most one distributor. Suppliers of the active ingredient, several markets and allocation between them are not modelled.
  • Production is continuous: campaigns, batch sizes and changeovers are averaged out.
  • The distributor's stock is not aged; shelf life is tracked at the plant only.
  • Nobody is laid off: the workforce shrinks only as people leave.
  • Costs and prices are illustrative, not a quotation.
